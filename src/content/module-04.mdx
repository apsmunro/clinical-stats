> **The running case — Snakeoilizumab**
>
> You have finally run a small two-arm randomised trial of a new blood-pressure drug, *Snakeoilizumab*,
> against placebo. 40 patients per arm. Your result: the treated group's mean systolic BP was
> **4 mmHg lower** than placebo. Is that a real effect, or just the luck of who happened to land
> in which arm? Everything below is about answering that question.

## Section 1 — The problem of the single sample

Except in rare circumstances, we can never be sure of the effect of a medication on *one individual*.
That is because we either give them a medication or not, and when we observe the effect we are unable
to compare what happened to them to the alternative since it never happened! This is what prevents us
from determining the impact of interventions in clinical practice; we have no *counterfactual* to
compare it to. Whilst we cannot usually do this with individuals, **we can create counterfactuals for groups.**
We can run trials with many people in them, then compare the totals or averages for each group to
try and create a counterfactual.

Now you ran the trial for snakeoilizumab once. You got one number: a difference in means of −4 mmHg.
But you didn't actually want to know about *these 80 people*. You wanted to know about blood-pressure
patients in general. You are trying to learn about a whole population from one small sample.

Here's the difficulty: even if Snakeoilizumab did *absolutely nothing*, you would almost
never get exactly 0 mmHg difference between the arms due to natural variation (remember - *variance is king*.)
Randomisation splits people into two groups, but the two groups are never identical: by pure chance one arm gets
slightly more high-BP patients than the other. That chance imbalance produces a difference in means
*all on its own*, with no drug effect at all.

So the real question isn't "is the difference exactly zero?" It's: **how big a difference could
chance alone plausibly produce, and is −4 mmHg bigger than that?**

To answer it, we need to know what "chance alone" looks like. And the cleanest way to *see* chance
alone is to simulate it.

## Section 2 — Building a sampling distribution

Let's make the thought experiment concrete. Suppose Snakeoilizumab truly has **no effect**. We can
imagine running the *same* trial over and over (fresh 40-vs-40 patients each time) and each time
writing down the difference in mean BP. After many repeats we'd have a whole *distribution* of
differences produced by chance alone. That distribution is called the **sampling distribution**,
and it is probably the most important idea in frequentist statistics. Remember, the whole philosophy
of frequentism is based on the idea that we could theoretically do what we have done in this simulation
and repeat the experiment over and over again, then draw inference from the patterns we would observe.

Three things to notice as you play, and they recur for the rest of the course:

1. **More repeats → a smoother, more stable shape.** With 50 trials it's lumpy; by 10,000 it's a
   clean bell-shaped normal distribution. The *shape* was always there. More data just reveals it.
2. **Bigger SD → a wider distribution.** Noisier patients mean chance can throw up bigger spurious
   differences. *Variance is king*: here's why it matters for inference, not just description.
3. **Bigger n → a narrower distribution.** More patients per arm pins down the mean more tightly,
   so chance differences shrink. This is the basis of Module 8 on power and sample size.

Why a bell-shape, by the way? Because each trial's result is an *average* of many patients, and averages of many independent values tend towards this shape, whatever the patients themselves look like. That's the **central limit theorem**, which underpins the inference in this module. (This is the explanation Module 2 promised.)

## Section 3 — The standard error

The width of that sampling distribution has a name: the **standard error (SE)**. It is simply the
standard deviation *of the sampling distribution*, a measure of how much your estimate would
bounce around if you repeated the study.

Don't confuse it with the standard deviation (SD):

- **SD** describes the spread of *individuals* in your data (how different patients are from each other).
- **SE** describes the spread of your *estimate* (how different your trial result would be on repetition).

For a difference in means, the SE shrinks as you collect more data and grows as the data get
noisier, as you just saw. A small, but very important detail is that the shrinkage
goes with the *square root* of n, not n itself. Averaging more patients does cancel noise, but the
errors only partially offset each other, so quadrupling the sample merely halves the SE. (This is
why big precision is expensive, and why Module 8's sample sizes climb so fast.) That single
relationship (*more n, less noise → smaller SE → sharper inference*) is the driving factor behind
confidence intervals, p-values, and statistical power alike.

## Section 4 — Confidence intervals

In real life you can't repeat the trial 10,000 times. You get **one** sample. The confidence
interval is the tool that applies the sampling-distribution idea to that single study. A 95% CI
is, roughly, your estimate give-or-take about two standard errors: this gives a range of possible values
for the true effect that would be compatible with your data.

Your trial would report: *−4 mmHg (95% CI −10.7 to +2.7)*. That's the observed difference,
give-or-take about two standard errors (2 × 3.4 ≈ 6.7).

This is a commonly misinterpreted object in medical statistics, so we treat the interpretation
carefully and *visually*. Below, you are allowed godlike knowledge for once: **you set the true effect**,
then run 100 trials and see where their confidence intervals fall relative to it.

<CICoveragePlot trueDiff={5} sd={15} n={40} nIntervals={100} seed={2001} />

**What a 95% confidence interval means:** if you repeated this study many times, about 95% of the
intervals you'd construct would contain the true effect. Confidence is a property of the
*procedure*, not of your one interval.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
A 95% CI does **not** mean "there's a 95% probability the true effect is between −10.7 and +2.7."
Once you've computed your specific interval, the true effect is either in it or it isn't: there's
no probability left to assign. (The "95% probability the value is in here" interpretation is the
*Bayesian credible interval*, which we will look at in Module 5, and contrasting the two is one of the
course's key learning objectives.)
</MisconceptionCallout>

A practical, less technical reading is to ignore the 95% part and treat the interval as "the range of true effects reasonably compatible with the result you observed." −10.7 to +2.7 includes a worthwhile drop, no effect, and a small rise, so this trial, on its own, is inconclusive.

## Section 5 — The p-value and NHST

Now we can finally define the mystical p-value properly, and you'll see it's just *reading off the sampling
distribution* from Section 2.

P values are most often generated from Null-hypothesis significance testing (NHST), which has a simple logic:

1. Assume the **null hypothesis** is true: here, the assumption in our testing is that Snakeoilizumab has no effect
   (true difference = 0). That's the sampling distribution centred on 0 you already built.
2. Ask: under that assumption, how often would chance alone produce a result **as extreme as, or
   more extreme than**, my observed −4 mmHg?
3. That probability is the **p-value**.

<TailAreaPlot sd={15} n={40} observedEffect={-4} tails={2} seed={3001} />

**The correct definition**, stated carefully:

> The p-value is the probability of observing data *at least as extreme as* what you saw,
> **if the null hypothesis were true**. In symbols, P(data | H₀).

Two common wrong definitions, both classic exam "trap" answers:

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconceptions">
- A p-value is **not** "the probability that the results are due to chance."
- A p-value is **not** "the probability that the null hypothesis is true." It says nothing directly
  about P(H₀ | data); for that you'd need Bayes (Module 5).

A small p-value means *your data would be surprising if there were no effect*. It does not mean
there's definitely an effect, and it does not say how *big* any effect is.
</MisconceptionCallout>

And the threshold (p \< 0.05) is a **convention, not a law of nature**: an arbitrary line Fisher
suggested, not a boundary between true and false. A p-value of 0.04 and 0.06 are almost the same
evidence; the cliff between "significant" and "not" is an arbitrary definition we impose for weak
historical reasons. Much has been written about the harm of splitting results into "significant" and "not significant" in medical statistics, and the debate is lively and worth reading.

## Section 6 — What inference does *not* do

Knowing these limits will help you avoid bad research. Statistical inference does **not**:

- **Prove causation.** That comes from study *design* (randomisation, control of confounding), not
  from any test. A tiny p-value in an observational study is still just an association.
- **Tell you whether an effect is clinically significant.** −4 mmHg can be "statistically
  significant" and clinically trivial, or non-significant yet potentially important but
  under-powered. Significance ≠ importance. (Importance is your judgement; the stats only
  quantify uncertainty.)
- **Tell you your data are any good.** Garbage in, garbage out: inference assumes your
  measurements, randomisation and follow-up were sound. It cannot rescue a biased study.

> *Inference quantifies uncertainty due to chance. It does not certify truth, importance, or
> quality; those are yours to argue for.*

## Optional R code

These mirror the code on the lecture slides, so live-course and self-study reinforce each other.
They're entirely optional; nothing later depends on them.

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Simulate the sampling distribution under the null"
  code={`set.seed(123)
n_patients    <- 40      # per arm
n_simulations <- 10000
true_diff     <- 0       # null: no effect
sd_bp         <- 15      # variance is king

mean_diffs <- numeric(n_simulations)
for (i in seq_len(n_simulations)) {
  control      <- rnorm(n_patients, mean = 120,             sd = sd_bp)
  intervention <- rnorm(n_patients, mean = 120 + true_diff, sd = sd_bp)
  mean_diffs[i] <- mean(intervention) - mean(control)
}

hist(mean_diffs, breaks = 50,
     main = "Sampling distribution under the null",
     xlab = "Difference in mean BP (mmHg)")
abline(v = -4, col = "red", lwd = 2)   # our observed result`}
/>

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Standard error, confidence interval and p-value the easy way"
  code={`# one simulated trial with a real 5 mmHg effect
set.seed(1)
control      <- rnorm(40, mean = 120, sd = 15)
intervention <- rnorm(40, mean = 115, sd = 15)

t.test(intervention, control)
# read off: estimate (difference in means),
#           95% CI, and p-value`}
/>

<CollapsibleRSnippet

  code={`mean(abs(mean_diffs) >= 4)   # two-tailed empirical p-value`}
