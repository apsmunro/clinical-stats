## Section 1 — Look before you test

Think of your dataset not as a spreadsheet but as 300 patients standing in a room.
The first step in any analysis, before any test, model, or comparison, is to *look at them*: plot the data, find the middle, measure the spread, note any unusual values. Many published statistical errors would have been caught by drawing a histogram early.

The histogram of your clinic's BPs peaks near 120 and tails off evenly on both sides. Many clinical measurements look like this (Module 4 explains why), but many don't: costs, lengths of stay and biomarker levels are often skewed, and summaries that work for a bell curve mislead for skewed data.

Different datasets can also have identical summary statistics. Flip between

## Section 2 — The middle: mean and median

Two everyday candidates for "the middle":

- The **mean**: add everything, divide by n. Uses every value; every value affects it.
- The **median**: line everyone up, take the middle person. Uses only *order*; the extremes can
  go as far as they like and the median won't budge.

For your clinic's symmetric BPs the two nearly coincide (~120), and the mean is the natural choice.
But now drag one patient's value upward (a transcription error, a single crisis admission, or any
other cause of an outlier) and watch what each does:

<OutlierDragDemo />

Neither is "better." They answer different questions: the mean is the centre of *mass* (and the
quantity trials usually compare: Module 4's "difference in means" is a difference of these); the
median is the *middle patient*. Which to use depends on the shape of the data and what the number is for.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"The average patient improved by 6 mmHg, so patients typically improve by about 6." Not
necessarily. If a minority improved enormously and most barely moved, the mean reports a typical
patient who doesn't exist.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 3 — The spread around the middle: SD, IQR and variance

Two patients in your clinic can differ by 40 mmHg while both are "around the average of 120." How
do we *quantify* "around"?

- **Standard deviation (SD)**: in plain language, **a typical distance from the mean.** It is calculated by taking each patient's distance from the mean, squaring it, averaging the squares, and taking the square root. Your clinic's SD of 15 mmHg means
  a randomly chosen patient usually sits within roughly ±15 of 120; a patient two SDs out
  (90 or 150) is unusual but far from impossible.
- The handy bell-curve rule: **mean ± 2 SD covers ~95%** of patients (±1 SD covers ~68%). For your
  clinic: 95% of BPs between roughly 90 and 150. The rule degrades as data get skewed, which is
  part of why shape matters.
- **IQR (interquartile range)**: the span of the middle half (25th to 75th percentile). Like the
  median, it ignores the extremes. It is the robust companion for skewed data.
- **Variance** is simply SD², the same information in squared units. This looks redundant, but variances *add* together in a way SDs don't, which is why statistical theory (and this course) works with variance.

Why does this course treat spread, rather than the average, as the most important quantity?

Because **every hard problem in clinical research is hard in proportion to the variation in it.**
If all patients responded identically, a trial would need two patients (one per arm) and no
statistics. It's because responses vary that one trial result could be chance (Module 4); it's
because patients vary that you need 142 of them per arm rather than 10 to see a 5 mmHg effect
(Module 8); it's the variation left over after your model has done its best that sets the precision
of every estimate you'll ever publish (Module 6). Two drugs with the same mean effect and different
spreads are *clinically different drugs*: one of them is reliable, the other more like a lottery.
If the "true" effect is the signal, then variance is what defines the noise we are trying to find it in.

This is the course's first catchphrase:

> **Variance is king.** The mean tells you the typical value. The variance decides how hard the true effect is to detect, and how many patients you need to detect it.

and watch more of its patients get no benefit while the average stays the same:

## Section 4 — Shape: when the standard summaries lie

"Mean ± SD" only tells the whole story for roughly symmetric, bell-shaped data. Common clinical shapes where it fails:

- **Right-skewed** (length of stay, costs, CRP, viral load): a long tail of large values drags the
  mean above the median; ±2 SD can include impossible values (negative lengths of stay) and
  misses where people actually are. *Report median + IQR.*
- **Outlier-contaminated** (data-entry errors, unit mix-ups, one extraordinary patient): a handful
  of points can move the mean and inflate the SD while 97% of the data sit unchanged. *Investigate
  first (outliers are sometimes the finding), then summarise robustly.*
- **Bimodal** (two humps, often two populations sharing one spreadsheet: treated and untreated,
  two diseases, two labs' assays): no single middle value describes the data. The histogram catches in one
  glance what no summary statistic can.

This brings us back to section 1: **plot first, summarise second.** Summary statistics are
compressions of information you can get in much higher resolution by just visualising your data.
(The five-shape widget back in Section 1 was exactly this warning: identical means, medians, *and*
SDs hiding wildly different distributions.)

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"Mean ± SD is the standard way to report data, so it's always fine." It's a *bell-curve* summary.
On skewed data it implies things that are false: symmetric spread, meaningful ±2 SD
coverage, a typical patient at the mean. Reviewers will likely read "mean ± SD" on an obviously
skewed variable as a sign that nobody plotted the data.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 5 — Robust data summaries (a field guide)

In practice:

1. **Plot first.** Histogram (or dot plot for small n). Every later choice follows from the shape.
2. **Bell-ish?** Mean ± SD, and say n. **Skewed?** Median + IQR. **Two humps?** Don't summarise.
   Split by the lurking subgroup and describe both.
3. **Always give the denominator and the spread.** "Responders: 7" means nothing without "of 22".
   "Mean improvement 6 mmHg" means little without an SD, *especially* in this course, because the SD appears in every later formula (Module 4's SE is the SD ÷ √n in disguise;
   Module 8's sample sizes scale with SD²).
4. **Beware the average patient.** Decisions get made for individuals drawn from the whole
   distribution, not for the mean. A drug that helps the average patient and harms a sizeable tail
   is not "a good drug, on average". It's a drug whose distribution you need to see.

One forward pointer, to head off a very common confusion in medical statistics: the SD describes the
spread and variation of individual *patients*. Module 4 introduces a different number, the standard
*error* (SE), which describes the spread of *study results*, or, "how much variation is there likely
to be between the results of this study and the *true result* I was trying to estimate". The SE
shrinks as n grows, and is **not** interchangeable with the SD, however often people confuse them.
We will come back to this in Module 4.

  caption="Describing the clinic population"
  code={`set.seed(2024)
bp <- rnorm(300, mean = 120, sd = 15)   # the clinic, simulated

mean(bp);  median(bp)
sd(bp);    IQR(bp)
hist(bp, breaks = 25, main = "The clinic's BPs", xlab = "Systolic BP (mmHg)")
abline(v = mean(bp), col = "red", lwd = 2)

# the ±2 SD rule of thumb:
