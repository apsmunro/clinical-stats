## Section 1 — The appraisal mindset

Module 1 named the three ways research misleads: **chance, bias, misinterpretation**. Appraisal looks for these three problems in a set order. Three habits
before any checklist:

- **Methods before Results.** The Methods section shows whether the study can be trusted.
  Decide what the study *could* show before reading what it claims to show. (Corollary: the
  abstract's conclusion sentence is the last thing you read, not the first.)
- **Find the comparison.** Every claim involves a comparison, stated or implied. Compared to whom?
  Randomised or self-selected (Module 6's confounding by indication)? If observational: what's on
  the adjustment list, and what obviously isn't?
- **Find the number that would matter.** Before judging what they found, fix what would be important: the
  MCID (Modules 1 and 8). A study can be impeccably analysed and still aimed at the wrong target.

Experienced readers don't work through a checklist from start to finish. They ask three questions in order, and move on only if the previous answer is satisfactory. This is the **three-step method**:

1. **What, precisely, was the question?** What did they try to find out, and why? *(Step 1)*
2. **Does this study answer it?** In two halves: was this the *best kind of study* that could
   answer the question — the gold-standard design — and was *this particular execution* clean
   enough to trust? *(Steps 2a and 2b)*
3. **Does this study apply to me?** Even a flawless answer to someone else's question may not
   transfer to your patients and your clinic. *(Step 3)*

The six-question checklist most of this module is focussed on lives entirely inside **Step 2b**. That's
where the statistics are key, so that's where we spend longest. Remember appraisals fail just as often at the steps on either side of it: a question you never quite pinned down, or an answer that doesn't fit the
patient in front of you.

## Section 2 — Step 1: What, precisely, was the question?

Get this wrong and everything downstream is wasted effort. A flawless answer to a vague or
mis-stated question tells you nothing, and you will be surprised how often it is hard to tell
what exactly the research team were trying to achieve, and why. Make sure you pin the question down before
you read another word of Results.

**For a trial, the question is PICO:** **P**atient (who, exactly — and how sick?),
**I**ntervention (what, at what dose, when?), **C**omparator (better than *what* — placebo, or the
current standard? superiority or non-inferiority?), **O**utcome (measured how, and when?). If the question doesn't fit PICO, the authors probably haven't defined it clearly.

**For an observational study, two distinctions determine what the study can show:**

- **Descriptive or inferential?** Counting what happened, or estimating a relationship? Different
  questions, different standards of proof.
- **Causal or predictive?** "Does the drug *cause* fewer events?" and "Does taking the drug
  *predict* fewer events?" are not the same question. Module 6 explained that the gap between them is confounding. A model can predict well and still tell you nothing about cause.

Then, always: **and why?** What decision hangs on the answer? That is where the **MCID** re-enters
(your §1 habit, "the number that would matter"): the question is never "is there an effect?" but
"is there an effect *big enough to act on*?". We've been using 5 mmHg since Module 1.

The three Snakeoilizumab papers ahead all address the *same* clinical question. *Does
Snakeoilizumab lower blood pressure (or cardiovascular events) by a margin worth having, versus the
realistic alternative?* Watch how differently each paper actually operationalises it. Much of appraisal is noticing when the question a study *answers* has drifted from the question it
*asks*.

## Section 3 — Step 2 (part a): Is this the best possible design?

Step 2 has two halves, and clinicians routinely skip the first. Before asking whether *this* study
was executed cleanly, ask whether it is even the *right kind of study* for the question. Each
question type has a gold-standard design. The appraisal move is to name it, then measure how far
the study in front of you falls short, and whether that shortfall was avoidable.

This matters: a registry used to answer a *treatment* question is not a slightly weaker RCT but the wrong design, and careful execution cannot fix that. The gold
standard for "does this treatment work?" is randomisation, and randomisation is exactly what it
lacks (Module 6's confounding by indication, structural and unfixable). When a study uses a weaker
design than the question allows, ask the question: *was this the best study that could have
been done, or just the easiest?* (Module 1: "feasibility is king, science gets shoe-horned in
afterwards.")

For the running case: ZENITH-BP and RAPID-BP are RCTs: the right tool for a treatment question, so
their problems (ahead) are problems of *execution*, Step 2b. SNAKE-REG is a registry pointed at a
treatment question, which is a Step 2a failure before we even reach its statistics. That distinction
changes what the flaws *mean*.

## Section 4 — Step 2 (part b): Does the execution hold up? (internal validity)

Say you have a clear question and the right design. Now the part this course has trained you for, young grasshopper: was *this execution* clean enough to believe? This is **internal validity**,
and it is where the statistics are fundamental. Each item is a way poor execution can undermine a good design:

1. **Who was compared, and was the comparison fair?** Randomised → chance imbalance is quantified
   by the machinery (Module 4). Observational → assume confounding until shown otherwise; adjustment
   models what was measured, never what wasn't (Module 6).
2. **What was measured, and how honestly is it summarised?** Right scale, right summary (mean ± SD
   vs median + IQR), denominators present, spread reported. *Variance is king*, so they should report it (Module 2).
3. **Powered for *what*?** Find the effect size in the power calculation. Is it a defensible MCID
   or a convenient number? No power statement at all is itself an answer (Modules 1, 8).
4. **What does the confidence interval say?** Not "significant or not", but *which effects remain
   compatible with the data*, and does that range include the MCID? A CI containing both "nothing"
   and "clearly worthwhile" means *inconclusive*, whatever the abstract says (Module 4).
5. **What is the effect in absolute terms?** Baseline risk, ARR, NNT. Relative numbers advertise;
   absolute numbers inform (Module 6; widget in Section 8).
6. **Any spin?** Small-n drama (winner's curse, Module 8), "trend toward significance", post-hoc
   power, subgroups discovered after the fact, OR quietly read as RR, causal verbs on observational
   data, a p-value asked to say Bayesian things it cannot (Modules 3, 5).

Against the checklist:

- **Question 3:** powered for **8 mmHg**. Your MCID has been 5 mmHg since Module 1. At n = 60 per
  arm the minimum detectable effect (80% power) is ~7.7 mmHg, so the study was *unable to detect* the difference that matters (Module 8's hidden underpowering).

- Verdict for the committee: *ZENITH-BP does not show the drug fails; it shows that a study which
  could not have caught a 5 mmHg effect, didn't.* Calling the drug "ineffective" misreads an absence of evidence.

## Section 8 — Relative vs absolute: the spin amplifier

Relative risk is the most common way to make results sound bigger. "Halves your risk" can mean a large benefit or a trivial one. **The baseline risk decides which**, and headlines usually leave it out. Start at the default (a 2% baseline), then drag the baseline to 40%: the same relative effect gives a 20-fold difference in absolute benefit.

<AbsoluteRelativeDemo defaultBaseline={2} defaultRr={0.5} />

The discipline: whenever you read a relative claim, **demand the baseline** and recompute.
ARR = baseline × (1 − RR); NNT = 100 ÷ ARR (in percentage points). It takes seconds, and most press releases look very different afterwards.

## Section 9 — The spin lexicon (a field guide)

Phrases that should make you suspicious. Each is a misuse covered earlier in the course:

- **"Trend toward significance" / "approaching significance"** (p = 0.08). p-values don't
  *approach*; the data are what they are. Either the CI is informative or it isn't (Module 4).
  (Curiously, p = 0.04 is never reported as "trend toward non-significance.")
- **"Post-hoc power was calculated…"** Circular; a re-expression of the p-value (Module 8). It usually appears because a reviewer asked for it.
- **"Significant in the subgroup of…"** Test enough subgroups after the fact and chance alone will produce a "responder population" (Module 4's false positives). Unless the subgroup was pre-specified, treat it as exploratory, not as evidence.
- **"Adjusted for [N] covariates"** as proof of causation. Adjustment only handles the confounders that were measured (Module 6).
- **"No difference between groups" (p > 0.05).** Show me the CI: no difference *demonstrated* is
  not no difference *present* (Modules 4, 8).
- **Causal verbs on observational nouns.** "Reduces", "protects", "prevents" attached to
  registries and cohorts. Only the study design can justify causal language (Modules 1, 6).

<MisconceptionCallout title="Reviewer's lens" variant="lens">
One reflex covers half this list: **ask what the authors *needed* to be true, then check whether
the analysis was given a chance to disagree.** Pre-specification, blinding, an MCID named in
advance, a CI displayed without comment: these are signs that the analysis could have produced an unwelcome result.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 10 — Step 3: Does this study apply to me? (external validity)

Pass Steps 1 and 2 and you have a trustworthy answer to *someone's* question, about *someone's*
patients. Step 3 asks whether it is *yours*. This is **external validity** (generalisability), and
no statistic certifies it. It is a clinical judgement, which is exactly why you have to make it yourself.
Three questions:

- **Are these patients like mine?** Check what someone had to *do to get into the study* — the
  eligibility criteria, the setting, the baseline severity. A trial in tertiary-centre patients
  with severe disease may say little about the mild cases filling your clinic.
- **Is the intervention feasible in my practice?** Could you actually deliver it — the dose, the
  monitoring, the measurement conditions — or did it lean on trial infrastructure you don't have?
- **Can I persuade people it matters?** The formulary committee meets Friday. An effect that is
  real, important, *and* applicable still has to be argued — in absolute terms (Section 8), against
  cost and the alternatives.

Now revisit the three papers through this lens and notice the trade-off it exposes:

- **ZENITH-BP** is a clean multicentre double-blind RCT, so *if* it had been informative its
  applicability would be decent. But it failed Step 2b: there is no trustworthy answer to apply.
  **Step 3 only matters once Step 2 is passed.** Don't reach for generalisability to rescue an
  uninformative study.
- **RAPID-BP** is single-centre and open-label, with BP read by ward staff at two weeks. Even
  granting an effect, would it reproduce outside that one centre, measured by people who knew which
  arm they were looking at? Narrow population and unreliable measurement: weak external and internal validity.
- **SNAKE-REG** is different. Its 4,800 real-world patients on real prescriptions are *exactly* the
  messy population you treat. Its **external validity is genuinely strong**, the one thing
  registries do better than tidy RCTs. Its internal validity is the weak part. That is a common
  trade-off: RCTs buy internal validity with narrow eligibility (strong Step 2b, fragile Step 3);
  observational studies buy a real-world population at the cost of confounding (strong Step 3,
  fragile Step 2b). The three-step method keeps you from mistaking one for the other.

## Section 11 — What statistics cannot certify (course summary)

Let us end where Module 1 began, with some boundaries clearly drawn. After nine modules, statistics can:
quantify what chance can fake (Module 4), update your beliefs with evidence (5), compare
like with like on what you measured (6), show how the named tests are one model (7), and tell you
before you start whether your study can answer its question (8). It cannot:

- **Certify causation.** Design does that (Modules 1, 4, 6).
- **Certify importance.** The MCID does that, and it's yours (Modules 1, 8).
- **Certify data quality.** Conduct does that (Module 1).
- **Certify applicability.** Whether the answer transfers to your patients is external validity
  (Step 3) and it lives in clinical judgement, not the confidence interval.
- **Replace judgement.** Nothing does. The numbers inform your judgement; they do not replace it.

> *A paper is a claim plus evidence. Statistics helps you assess the evidence. Whether you accept the claim is still your decision.*

One last piece of advice: **befriend a statistician, ideally
before data collection.** Everything in Modules 1–9 makes that conversation better, but nothing in
them replaces it.

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Converting an OR to risks, ARR and NNT"
  code={`# "Halves cardiovascular risk!" - OR 0.55, but events are common (30%)
p0   <- 0.30                       # baseline risk (non-users)
or   <- 0.55
odds0 <- p0 / (1 - p0)
p1   <- (odds0 * or) / (1 + odds0 * or)

p1                # 0.19  -> treated risk
p1 / p0           # 0.64  -> the actual risk ratio ("halves" was the OR)
p0 - p1           # 0.11  -> ARR: 11 percentage points
ceiling(1 / (p0 - p1))   # NNT: 1/0.109 = 9.2 -> round up: 10`}
/>
