## Section 1 — One model, three dials

Module 6 said that once you know the linear model, the named tests follow. This module shows how:

**Almost every classical statistical test you have heard of is the linear model**
`outcome = a + b × predictor + noise` **with three dials set differently.**

The rest of this module works through the three dials:

1. **What is the predictor?** Continuous (→ correlation, regression) or categorical (→ t-test, ANOVA).
   A categorical predictor is just turned into 0/1 "dummy" columns, exactly as we did with `treated`
   in Module 6.
2. **Do we use the data, or its ranks?** Run the model on the raw numbers and you get the familiar
   "parametric" tests. Run the *identical* model on the **ranks** of the data and you get the "non-parametric" tests.
3. **What link connects the line to the outcome?** For a normal outcome, none (the line *is* the
   prediction). For a yes/no outcome, the logit link from Module 6's logistic regression. For counts,
   a log link. This is the step up to **generalised linear models (GLMs)**.

Set the three dials and you get the named test. The names (Pearson, Student, Fisher, Wilcoxon) come from before cheap computing, when each special case needed its own shortcut formula. Now you only need the model and the three dials.

Why does this matter? Because it means **you already understand every one of these
tests.** Everything from Module 4 (standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values) and Module 6
(coefficients, adjustment) applies to all of them at once. And it means you can always ask the
clinically useful question, *how big is the effect, and how uncertain?*, instead of stopping at
"the Mann–Whitney test was significant (p = 0.03)", which tells a patient nothing.

## Section 2 — A continuous predictor: Pearson's r is just a slope

Start with **Pearson's correlation coefficient, r.** Most people know it runs from −1 to +1; fewer know what it measures.

Take two continuous variables, say each patient's **baseline BP** and their **age**. Module 6
already taught you to fit a line, `BP = a + b × age`, where the slope `b` is in real units: mmHg per
year. Pearson's r is *that exact same fit*, with one change: **put both variables in
standard-deviation units first** (subtract the mean, divide by the SD — the "z-scores" from Module 2).

Once both axes are measured in SDs, the slope is no longer "mmHg per year"; it is "SDs of BP per SD
of age", a pure, unit-free number. And that number is **r**. It tells you how many SDs the outcome changes for each SD change in the predictor. In short:

> **Pearson's r is the slope of the regression line when both variables are standardised.**

This is why r is bounded at ±1 (a slope steeper than 1 SD-per-SD would predict more variation than
exists), why r = 0 means a flat line, and why the p-value from a correlation test is *identical* to the
p-value for the slope in a linear regression — they are the same test. The correlation isn't a different tool from
regression; it's regression without the units.

unit-free −1…+1 score. Regression is usually the more useful of the two, because "0.6 mmHg per
year" answers a question a clinician can act on, while "r = 0.42" does not.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 3 — A categorical predictor: the t-test and ANOVA are one model

Module 6 showed that comparing **two** groups with a t-test is the linear model
`outcome = a + b × treated`, where `treated` is a 0/1 dummy: `a` is the placebo mean and `b` is the
difference. Nothing new there. The question this section answers is: what happens with **three or
more** groups? That's the situation people reach for **ANOVA** (analysis of variance).

You add more dummy columns. With our three arms (placebo, low dose,
high dose), pick one as the **reference** (placebo) and create two 0/1 indicators:

> **outcome = a + b₁ × (low dose?) + b₂ × (high dose?) + noise**

- `a` (the intercept) is the **placebo mean**.
- `b₁` is **how much low dose differs from placebo**.
- `b₂` is **how much high dose differs from placebo**.

Each patient is in exactly one arm, so for a low-dose patient the high-dose indicator is 0, and the
model just reads off `a + b₁`. This 0/1 bookkeeping is called **dummy coding**, and it is how a categorical predictor is put into an equation built for numbers.

So where is "ANOVA"? The classic one-way ANOVA F-test asks a single yes/no question: **are any of the
group differences real, or could the whole spread of group means be chance?** In linear-model terms
that is exactly: *are b₁ and b₂ both zero?* The ANOVA F-statistic and the F-test that the linear model reports
for the same model are **the identical number**. ANOVA is not a separate procedure — it is the linear
model's "is any of this predictor doing anything?" test, named in the 1920s and usually taught as a separate method.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"ANOVA tells you which groups differ." It doesn't. The F-test only says that at least one group differs. Which
ones, and by how much, are the coefficients (and their confidence intervals), which the linear model reports and ANOVA does not. This is why "ANOVA was significant" should always be followed by looking at the actual group differences.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 4 — Non-parametric tests: the same model, run on ranks

This dial is the most surprising. The "non-parametric" tests that you're told to use
when your data are skewed or have outliers, are not a separate family of methods. They are the linear
models you already know, **run on the ranks of the data instead of the data itself.**

A **rank transform** is simple: line the values up smallest to largest and replace each one by its
position (1, 2, 3, …). The smallest length-of-stay becomes 1, the next becomes 2, and the patient who was an extreme outlier, pulling every mean upwards, becomes just
"the biggest", say 60 out of 60. Ranks keep the *order* but discard the *spacing*, which is precisely
why outliers and skew stop doing damage: an extreme value can only ever take the highest rank.

Two named tests fall straight out:

- **Spearman's correlation** is **Pearson's r computed on the ranks.** This is the definition, not an analogy. Rank both variables, run Section 2's standardised slope, and you have Spearman's ρ. It
  measures *monotonic* association (does y tend to rise with x?) rather than strictly *straight-line*
  association, which is why curves and outliers affect it less.
- **The Mann–Whitney U test** (and its paired cousin, the **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**) is very nearly **the two-group comparison from Section 3 run on ranks**: `lm(rank(outcome) ~ group)`.
  Rank everyone's length of stay ignoring their arm, then ask whether one arm's ranks sit
  systematically higher. That is a t-test on ranks.

Watch it happen. Below is a skewed outcome where a couple of outliers distort the raw comparison. Press "rank-transform" and the values move to their rank positions; the relationship straightens (for
Spearman) or the groups separate cleanly (for Mann–Whitney), and the *same* linear model now fits:

<RankTransformDemo seed={7401} />

**Exact versus approximate.** Spearman = Pearson
on ranks is **exact** (it's the definition). The Mann–Whitney/Wilcoxon ≈ "t-test on ranks" equivalence
is a **very close approximation** rather than an algebraic identity. The classic rank-sum statistic and
the linear model on ranks give all-but-identical answers for any sample size you'd actually trust, but
they aren't computed by literally the same formula. The teaching point remains the same; there is no separate non-parametric method, just the linear model applied to ranks.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"Non-parametric tests have no assumptions." They have fewer, not none, and they answer a slightly
different question (about ranks/order, roughly the median, rather than the mean). The price of
robustness is that you get a result in *rank* units, which is why a Mann–Whitney p-value should still
be paired with an interpretable effect (a median difference, or the estimate from the rank model),
never reported alone. Reaching for ranks "to be safe" when the data are actually fine just throws
away information and power.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 5 — When the outcome isn't a normal number: GLMs

The last dial handles outcomes that aren't continuous bell-curved numbers at all: **yes/no** outcomes,
**counts**, **rates**. The linear model `a + b × x` can predict any number from −∞ to +∞, which is
fine for blood pressure but nonsense for a probability (stuck between 0 and 1) or a count (never
negative). The fix, which you already met in Module 6, is to keep the familiar straight-line
**linear predictor** and pass it through a **link function** that maps it onto the right scale.

This is the **generalised linear model (GLM)**, and it has two parts:

> **link( expected outcome ) = a + b × x** &nbsp;&nbsp;(the linear predictor — unchanged)

- **Normal outcome → identity link** ("do nothing"). This is ordinary linear regression: everything in Module 6 and the sections above. The plain linear model is just the simplest GLM.
- **Binary outcome → logit link.** This is **logistic regression** from Module 6 §5: the log-odds run
  −∞…+∞ (so a straight line is safe), and back-transforming gives an odds ratio. So logistic regression was a GLM all along.
- **Count outcome → log link.** This is **Poisson regression**, for things like number of exacerbations
  per year. The log keeps predictions positive, and `exp(b)` is a **rate ratio**.

Flip between the links below. The straight line in "link space" is identical each time; what changes is
how it bends back onto the outcome scale — the same predictor, three different shapes:

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"Logistic and Poisson regression are advanced, separate techniques." They are the *same* linear model
with a different link, fitted by the same software with one extra argument (`family =` in R). If you
understand `a + b × x` and the idea of a link that keeps predictions sensible, you understand GLMs.
The coefficients just need back-transforming (`exp`) into the odds ratios and rate ratios that
journals report.
</MisconceptionCallout>

Two practical benefits:

1. **You never have to memorise a test-selection flowchart again.** Ask three questions: 1) what's my
   predictor, 2) do I need ranks, 3) what kind of outcome, and the linear model tells you which classical
   test you're implicitly running. Test-selection flowcharts encode this same table.
2. **Every one of these tests gives you an effect size and a confidence interval**, because every one
   is a linear model with coefficients (Module 4's machinery, Module 6's interpretation). Module 9 relies on this: don't stop at "the test was significant"; report how big the effect is and how uncertain. That is what a patient needs.

> *There was never a zoo of unrelated tests. There was one model, and a century of people giving its
> special cases different names. Variance is still king; this is just the king's whole court, finally
> introduced as one family. Perhaps I am the Jester...*

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Spearman IS Pearson on the ranks"
  code={`cor.test(x, y, method = "spearman")        # Spearman's rho
cor.test(rank(x), rank(y))                 # Pearson on ranks: same rho
summary(lm(rank(y) ~ rank(x)))             # ...and it's a linear model`}
/>

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="t-test and one-way ANOVA are linear models"
  code={`group <- factor(rep(c("placebo", "low", "high"), each = 20))
y     <- rnorm(60, mean = c(120, 116, 112)[group], sd = 15)

summary(aov(y ~ group))        # the ANOVA F-test
anova(lm(y ~ group))           # identical F-test from the linear model
summary(lm(y ~ group))         # intercept = placebo mean; coefficients = differences`}
/>

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Mann–Whitney is (almost exactly) a t-test on ranks"
  code={`g2 <- factor(rep(c("placebo", "drug"), each = 30))

wilcox.test(los ~ g2)                        # Mann–Whitney U
summary(lm(rank(los) ~ g2))                  # the same comparison on ranks`}
/>
