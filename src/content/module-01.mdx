## Section 1 — The patient who got better

A colleague corners you at coffee. "I've started three patients on Snakeoilizumab. One of them:
BP down 18 mmHg in two weeks. *Eighteen.* This stuff works."

Statistics is needed because **patients vary, even without treatment.** Blood pressure bounces around within the same person from week to week (measurement
noise, stress, sleep, salt, regression to the mean after a high reading). Across people, the same
disease ranges from mild to severe. Some of your untreated patients will improve dramatically
*this month*, on nothing at all.

So one patient improving a lot is not evidence that the drug works. Untreated patients improve this much all the time.

Anecdotes are not lies: the patient really did improve. But random variation alone produces some **impressive individual results**, so a single story, however sincere, cannot tell you whether the *drug* did anything. For that you need the whole distribution and a fair comparison. That is what this course is about.

## Section 2 — Three ways to be wrong

When a study (or a coffee-room claim) misleads, the cause is almost always one of three things.
Keeping them apart is a huge part of critical appraisal:

1. **Chance.** Random variation produced a difference that means nothing. (Whole groups can also differ by luck; Module 4 covers this.) *Statistics cannot stop chance, but it can measure how large a role chance could have played.*
2. **Bias.** The comparison was unfair, though not necessarily on purpose. The treated patients
   were younger, or sicker, or measured differently, or only the responders came back to clinic.
   *Statistics can describe and sometimes adjust (Module 6), but the real fix is design:
   randomisation, blinding, complete follow-up.* No analysis rescues a broken design.
3. **Misinterpretation.** The data were fine; the reading of them wasn't. "Significant" mistaken
   for "important", a confidence interval interpreted as a probability, a non-significant result read as
   "no effect". *The fix is understanding what the numbers mean. Modules 4, 5 and 8 cover the common mistakes.*

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"Statistics can rescue a bad study." It cannot. Statistics quantifies the uncertainty *due to
chance*, on the assumption that the data collection was sound. If the comparison was biased, even the best analysis only gives you a precise estimate of the wrong thing. Garbage in, garbage out.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 3 — What statistics actually is (and isn't)

Without the notation, statistics comes down to one idea: **before believing a difference is real, work out how big a difference chance alone could produce.** Almost everything in Modules 4–8 (standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values, power) is a way of answering that question.

Two things statistics is *not*:

- **Not a source of certainty.** No test proves the drug works. Every statistical result is uncertain and depends on assumptions; what statistics gives you is a measure of that uncertainty.
- **Not a yes/no stamp.** Treating "p \< 0.05" as a yes/no verdict at the end of a study is one of the most damaging habits in medical research, because it hands the decision to an arbitrary cut-off. (Module 4 shows where 0.05 came from; Module 8 shows what the ritual costs.) The
  numbers inform a judgement, but the judgement needs to be made by you.

One reassurance: **the mathematics in this course is almost
entirely optional.** Every key idea comes with an interactive picture (a slider, a simulation, a histogram). If you can judge how far to trust a weather forecast, you can follow all of it. The purpose of this course is not to understand formulae, but to understand
the intuition and meaning behind what these formulae are trying to achieve, and what the results mean.
*If you are using numbers to answer your research question, you need to understand what the numbers mean*.

## Section 4 — The number that should come first

Every study should start with this question, and few do:

> **How big a difference would actually matter to patients?**

Not "how big a difference do we expect" (optimism), and not "how big a difference can we detect
with the patients we can get" (convenience). *How big a difference would change what a clinician
does or how a patient fares?* For Snakeoilizumab, plausibly something like a **5 mmHg** systolic
reduction would be important: less than that, side-effects, cost and pill burden probably outweigh
the gain; at or above it, you and the patients would want to know.

That number is the **minimum clinically important difference (MCID)**. It is **a clinical judgement, not a statistical output.** No formula produces it; it comes from your knowledge of the disease, the patients and the alternatives. Module 6 places it alongside other effect sizes, and Module 8 builds study design around it.

It appears this early because it protects against a common mistake:

<MisconceptionCallout title="The methodology theme of this course" variant="lens">
**"Feasibility is king, science gets shoe-horned in afterwards."** The most common way clinical
research goes wrong is not fraud or incompetence. It's a study built around what was *convenient*
(the patients available, the clinic's database, the registrar's year) with the scientific question
trimmed to fit. It asks, "what can I achieve with what is easily available?", instead of,
"what do I need to answer the question that really matters?". The result is studies that use respectable methods but could never have answered their question. Doing it the right way round
(question first, then the MCID, then a design big and clean enough to detect it, as Module 8 shows)
is the most important habit this course aims to teach.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 5 — How this course works

Course outline:

- **Module 2 — Describing data.** Before any inference: means, medians, spread, and why
  *variation* is the central character of the whole story.
- **Module 3 — Two philosophies of probability.** "Probability" means two different things, and the two main schools of statistics grew from them.
- **Module 4 — From sample to inference.** Sampling distributions, standard
  errors, confidence intervals, p-values. Run the Snakeoilizumab trial 10,000 times and watch
  chance at work.
- **Module 5 — Thinking Bayesian.** The question you actually wanted to ask ("what's the
  probability it works?") and what it costs to answer.
- **Module 6 — The linear model.** One model behind the menu of named tests; adjustment,
  confounding, and where effect sizes live.
- **Module 7 — One model, many tests.** Correlation, t-tests, ANOVA, the rank-based tests and GLMs are all versions of the linear model.
- **Module 8 — Power & sample size.** The design-time module: how the MCID sets the sample size, and why underpowered studies mislead.
- **Module 9 — Critical appraisal.** Applying the whole course to published papers.

Each module is interactive-first: play with the widget, then read why it behaved that way. R code
snippets are always optional, always collapsed, never required, but useful if you want to develop
your skills.
