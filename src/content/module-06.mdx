## Section 1 — You've been using a linear model all along

Here is Module 4's t-test, written as an equation instead of a test. **Tap each coloured part of
the equation** to see what it means and what it does to the data:

<EquationAnatomy seed={6001} />

where `treated` is 0 for placebo and 1 for the drug arm. Two coefficients: `a` is the mean BP of
the placebo group; `b` is *the difference the drug makes*, and for our trial, b = **−4 mmHg**. It gives exactly the same estimate, standard error and p-value as Module 4's t-test. A t-test is a linear model with one binary predictor. (The first R snippet at the
bottom of this page shows this in more detail).

I promised no equations, but this one is very important. If you can understand the basic machinery
inside of the linear model, you are able to apply this to almost **all common statistical tests**
to understand what is happening inside them. No more consulting flow charts or memorising categories,
you can apply your understanding of *this one test* to almost all the others. Let's take a minute to
slow down and explain each part of the equation:

The left hand side starts with the result or outcome. The remainder of the equation beyond the "="
sign explains how we are going to work it out. This is sometimes labelled as **y** (as it is usually
plotted on the y axis).

Next we have our reference point, sometimes called the "intercept" as this is where the regression
line will "intercept" the y axis. This is where we begin when our predictor variable is set at zero.
This can literally be zero when it is a continuous variable, or else a figurative zero when there are
two categories and we use zero to indicate this is the reference category. This is sometimes labelled
as **a** or alpha, or beta zero. Why? Because statisticians love Greek letters and hate making things
obvious.

Next is the effect size, called the **coefficient**. It is the expected change in your outcome for a one-unit increase in your predictor variable (or, with two groups, for moving from the reference category to the intervention category). This is usually labelled as **beta 1**.

Next, we multiply our effect size (beta 1) by the unit of our predictor, or
zero/one for whether we are in the reference category (zero) or intervention category (one). This
predictor variable is usually labelled as **x** (as it is usually plotted on the x axis).

Finally, we add in a variable for noise. This is simply to show that we don't expect our predictor
variable or categories to perfectly explain all the variation in the outcome variable, so an extra
variable is added to mop up all the random noise and variation added by all the other unmeasured
things which contribute to the outcome variable. Sometimes this is labelled as **e** or epsilon.

Take some time making sure you are comfortable with each part of this equation. For the reasons
mentioned above, if you can comfortably explain what is going on in the linear model, you will have no
difficulty understanding how all the other common statistical tests work. They are almost all
variations of this model or special cases of how it is used.

But why bother using this instead of our good old-fashioned t-test anyway? Because the equation form
*scales* and the test form doesn't. Want age in the picture? Add `+ c × age`. Baseline BP?
`+ d × baseline`. A dose instead of a yes/no? Let the predictor be continuous. Every extension of the
question is one more term in the same equation, whereas the named tests (t-test, ANOVA, ANCOVA, correlation…) are all versions of this one model. Learn it once and the rest follows.

## Section 2 — Lines through data: fitting by least squares

That was the example of having two groups. We mentioned making the predictor continuous before, so
let's take a look at that now. In the placebo arm, does systolic BP rise with age? Plot BP against
age: a cloud of points with an upward drift. The linear model proposes a line:

> **BP = a + b × age + noise**

`a` is the intercept (BP at age 0, often a mathematical convenience rather than a clinical claim)
and `b` is the slope: **mmHg per year of age**. If b = 0.6, two
patients ten years apart differ by ~6 mmHg on average.

Which line is *best*? For each candidate line, every point has a **residual**: the vertical gap
between observed and predicted. This measures how much our prediction missed what the true values
were at each point on the line. Squaring and summing the residuals gives us a quantifiable score; the
**least-squares** line is the one with the smallest total. That's the whole fitting procedure: make the misses as small as possible. (Why *squared*? Two reasons worth knowing. Squaring punishes
big misses disproportionately: one 10 mmHg miss costs as much as a hundred 1 mmHg misses. And it
makes the best line a *mean-like* object, the same centre-of-mass logic as Module 2's mean, which
is also why outliers tug on it the same way.)

Your turn to be the fitting algorithm. Drag the line to chase the smallest Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)
you can, then check yourself against the least-squares button. Then switch to *drag points* mode and pull an
extreme-age patient far off the line. The best-fit line moves towards it: points at the edges have *leverage*.

<DraggableRegression seed={3001} />

One more piece of vocabulary while the residuals are on screen: their standard deviation is the
**residual SD**, the noise left over after the model has explained what it can. It comes back in Section 6 and in Module 8.

## Section 3 — Coefficients come with uncertainty (Module 4 applies)

A slope estimated from 25 patients is an *estimate*, exactly as Module 4's −4 mmHg was. Re-run the
study and the fitted slope wobbles. Try the **"Re-sample 25 patients"** button above after showing
the least-squares fit, and watch the fitted slope change with each fresh sample. A coefficient has
a **sampling distribution**, hence a **standard error**, hence a **95% CI** and a **p-value**. All
of Module 4's machinery applies to every coefficient in any regression, unchanged:

- *"b = 0.6, 95% CI (0.2 to 1.0)"*: the range of slopes compatible with the data. It excludes 0,
  so age and BP are associated beyond plausible chance.
- The p-value for a coefficient tests against a null hypothesis for the coefficient; "true coefficient = 0",
  i.e. *this predictor adds nothing*, and carries every caveat from Module 4 (it is not the probability
  the predictor matters, significance is not importance, and a CI is more informative than a star).

Nothing in this section is new. That's the point: Module 4 taught inference for one number;
regression produces several numbers, each following the same rules. (And if you'd rather have
posterior distributions for coefficients instead, Module 5 applies wholesale too. Same model,
either inferential lens.)

## Section 4 — Adjustment: comparing like with like

Now we will look at where understanding the nuts and bolts becomes particularly important in
clinical reading.

Your colleague runs an **observational** study of Snakeoilizumab: no randomisation, because
clinicians prescribed it to whoever they judged needed it. Unsurprisingly, treated patients started
*sicker* (higher baseline severity). At follow-up, the crude comparison shows: **treated
patients did no better, maybe worse, than untreated.** Does the drug fail outside trials?

Severity is a **confounder**: it pushed patients *into* treatment and *also* worsened
their outcomes. The crude comparison mixes the drug's effect with the severity gap between the
groups. The fix is the equation form from Section 1:

> **outcome = a + b × treated + c × severity + noise**

Adding severity to the model asks a sharper question: *among patients of the same severity, what
does treatment change?* The coefficient "b" is now the **adjusted** treatment effect.

See it happen. The simulation below builds in a true drug benefit of −4 mmHg, then lets severity
drive prescribing. Without adjustment, the drug looks useless. Turn on adjustment to compare like with like. Then drag the confounding-strength slider to zero and notice crude ≈ adjusted: *that is what randomisation achieves.*

<AdjustmentDemo seed={6006} />

Three caveats, because adjustment is often over-trusted:

- **You can only adjust for what you measured.** Unmeasured confounding survives any number of
  covariates. ("We adjusted for everything" means "everything we thought of and recorded.")
- **Adjustment does not prove causation.** It compares like with like *on the included
  variables*, under the model's assumptions. Randomisation (Module 4) balances *everything*,
  measured and unmeasured, which is why it remains the gold standard and why this course keeps
  repeating "causation comes from design, not analysis."
- **Adjusting for the wrong thing can hurt.** Adjusting for a variable on the causal path (a
  mediator), or for a consequence of treatment, can *create* bias rather than remove it. The rule
  of thumb at this level: adjust for pre-treatment confounders, not post-treatment variables.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"The study adjusted for 15 variables, so the association is probably causal." Adjustment is only as
good as the list, and the list never includes what nobody measured (or imagined). A heavily
adjusted observational estimate is still an observational estimate. Even if you think you have
adjusted for every prognostic variable, there can still be large amounts of residual confounding due
to errors in how these were measured, misclassified, etc. Design (randomisation) removes
confounding; adjustment merely *models* the part of it you caught.
</MisconceptionCallout>

Adjustment also helps in **randomised** trials. Adjusting for baseline BP doesn't fix confounding (randomisation already did); it **reduces unexplained variation**, shrinking the residual SD and narrowing the CI for the treatment effect. This is essentially a free way of reducing
the uncertainty in your estimate. Remember Module 8's option 2 for the unaffordable sample size,
*reduce the variance*? This is the analysis-side version of that lever.

## Section 5 — How to estimate effect sizes for binary outcomes

A binary outcome ("controlled BP at 12 weeks": yes/no) can't sit raw on the left of a linear
equation, because predictions must stay between 0% and 100%. You can't have a probability of
a fixed outcome that is more than 1 or less than zero, this breaks the maths and is nonsense.
The solution is to change the scale:
**logistic regression** models the *log-odds* of the outcome:

> **log-odds(controlled) = a + b × treated + c × severity + …**

Same right-hand side, same adjustment logic, same coefficient machinery (SEs, CIs, p-values:
Module 4 again). The only novelty is the **scale of the coefficients**: back-transformed, `exp(b)`
is an **odds ratio (OR)**, the multiplicative change in the odds of the outcome with treatment.
The simple way to think of it: taking the log-odds transforms probabilities of 0 and 1 to minus infinity and infinity. This turns a scale with fixed ends into one with no ends, so a straight line can be fitted to it.

<LogOddsTransform />

Suppose 40% of placebo patients achieve control versus 55% on treatment (the worked example
Module 8 will size a study around; the smallest such difference worth caring about is what
Module 8 will name the **MCID**, the number every sample-size calculation should be anchored to).
The same comparison can be expressed three ways:

- **Risk difference**: 15 percentage points. The absolute measure; drives NNT (≈ 1/0.15 ≈ 7) and
  powers sample-size maths.
- **Risk ratio**: 0.55/0.40 ≈ 1.4, i.e. "40% more likely to achieve control". Intuitive, but not
  what logistic regression outputs.
- **Odds ratio**: (0.55/0.45)/(0.40/0.60) ≈ 1.8. This is what logistic regression *does* output,
  and the one journals are full of.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"An odds ratio of 1.8 means patients are 1.8× as likely to achieve control." That's the *risk
ratio* reading, and here the RR is 1.4, not 1.8. ORs exaggerate relative to RRs whenever the
outcome is common (ours is ~50%!); they approximate RRs only for rare outcomes. When you read
"OR = 1.8", reach for the baseline risk before translating it into anything a patient would
recognise, and notice the absolute difference (15 points) is the number that drives clinical
decisions and sample sizes alike.
</MisconceptionCallout>

(If you want to play with the three measures, the binary mode of Module 8's sample-size calculator
interconverts them live.)

## Section 6 — Assumptions, residuals and variance

Every model reaches its conclusions using assumptions. The linear model's, in plain language and in
rough order of how much they matter:

1. **Independence.** Patients' errors don't influence each other. The most important, and the hardest to check from plots (clustered data such as wards or repeated measures need extended models, the
   "clustering" Module 8 warns about in sample-size planning).
2. **Linearity.** The relationship is adequately straight over the observed range. Check:
   residuals-vs-fitted plot. Fix: transformations or curves, same family.
3. **Constant variance** (homoscedasticity). Noise is similar across the range, e.g., residuals
   aren't smaller when your predictors are smaller and larger when they are larger, etc.
4. **Normal residuals.** *Least important, most worried-about.* Note carefully: the assumption is
   about **residuals**, being normally distributed around the regression line, not the raw outcome,
   itself being normally distributed. With decent sample sizes the central limit theorem (Module 4) makes coefficient inference robust to non-normality anyway.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"My outcome variable isn't normally distributed, so I can't use a linear model." The model never
required the *outcome* to be normal, only the residuals, approximately, and even that softens with
n. Skewed outcomes often yield perfectly well-behaved residuals once predictors are in the model.
Check the residual plot, not the outcome histogram.
</MisconceptionCallout>

One of the important things about the assumptions, is that they are almost never all met perfectly
in any model. For example, there is almost nothing in biology that meets the assumption of a
linear relationship — things usually fit more of an "S shaped" curve. However, we can accept that
the linear model is almost always wrong, but almost always good enough to be useful. In fact, it is very useful for such a simple model. Don't get too hung up over the
assumptions unless something is clearly grossly violated. Even then the situation may be salvageable.

To summarise: the **residual SD** is the variation the model couldn't explain. It is *the* SD in Module 4's standard error, the SD that widened every
sampling distribution, and the SD in Module 8's sample-size formula. Good covariates shrink it; sloppy
measurement inflates it. A measure you may have heard of, **R²** ("share of variance explained")
is related and worth checking, but a model can have a low R² and still estimate a
treatment effect precisely, and a high R² proves nothing about causation. The residual SD, not R²,
is what feeds back into design.

> *Variance is king. The linear model explains what variation it can, and what's left (the residual) determines how precise every estimate will be.*

treated <- rep(c(0, 1), each = 40)
bp      <- rnorm(80, mean = 120 - 4 * treated, sd = 15)

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Adjustment: crude vs adjusted treatment effect (confounding by indication)"
  code={`set.seed(42)
n         <- 120
severity  <- runif(n, 0, 10)
treated   <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(severity - 5))  # sicker -> more often treated
bp_change <- -4 * treated + 1.5 * severity + rnorm(n, sd = 4)
# true drug effect: -4 mmHg; severity makes BP fall less (or rise)

coef(lm(bp_change ~ treated))             # crude: ~0, drug looks useless
coef(lm(bp_change ~ treated + severity))  # adjusted: ~ -4 mmHg recovered`}
/>

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Binary outcomes: logistic regression and the odds ratio"
  code={`controlled <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.4 + 0.6 * treated))
fit <- glm(controlled ~ treated, family = binomial)

exp(coef(fit))["treated"]     # odds ratio
exp(confint(fit))             # 95% CI for the OR
# absolute risks are still the clinical currency:
tapply(controlled, treated, mean)`}
