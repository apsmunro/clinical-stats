## Section 1 — Why this matters in practice

It is tempting to file *"what does probability actually mean?"* under philosophy: academically interesting,
but safe to skip. I promise you it is not! This is one of the most practical questions in the whole course,
so let's walk through why.

**Every number a clinical study hands you as a result is written in the language of probability.**
When you read a p-value, a confidence interval, a *"significant"* result, or a hazard ratio with its confidence interval, each one is a probability statement: the result **is** the probability. If you don't know what that probability means, you don't know what your result means.

One of the most common errors in evidence-based medicine is reading
a probability of one philosophy as though it were the other: hearing *"the probability of data this
extreme if the drug does nothing is 3%"* and concluding *"there's a 97% chance the drug works."*
Those are different sentences with different meanings, and confusing them has led to ineffective treatments being adopted and effective ones being abandoned.

The fix is conceptual, not mathematical: learn that "probability" has two meanings, and learn to recognise which one is being used. That is what this module covers.

## Section 2 — One word, two meanings

The following three sentences all use the same word:

1. *"This coin has a 50% **probability** of heads."*
2. *"In this programme, the **probability** that a positive screen is a true positive is 8%."*
3. *"There's a 30% **probability** this patient's chest pain is cardiac."*

Sentences 1 and 2 describe **repeatable situations**: flip the coin a thousand times, screen a
thousand patients, and the probability is just the proportion you'd see in the long run. You could,
in principle, *check it by counting*.

Sentence 3 is different in kind. This patient's pain is cardiac or it isn't; there's no population
of identical chest-pains to count. The 30% is not a property of the world; it measures *your* **uncertainty**, given what you know. And yet it's not arbitrary. It should move
with evidence (an abnormal ECG should raise it), and a clinician whose 30%s come true about 30% of
the time is obviously doing something right!

Both meanings are coherent and useful, but they are not the same:

- **Frequentist probability**: a property of the *world*, the long-run frequency of an outcome
  under repetition.
- **Bayesian probability**: a property of *your knowledge*, a degree of belief about a fact,
  updated as evidence arrives.

Make sure you are comfortable with the above definitions, as they are important for the remainder of the module and the course.

## Section 3 — The long run (and how short the short run is)

The **law of large numbers** explains many features of frequentist statistics: as repetitions increase, the observed proportion gets closer to the true probability. The catch is that this happens **slowly**, and early results can be misleading.

Three things to take from playing with it:

1. **Small samples vary a lot.** Ten patients routinely yield response rates of 20% or 80% when
   the truth is 50%. Nothing went wrong, that *is* randomness. Remember this, as it's important for
   Module 4's sampling variation and Module 8's case for adequate sample sizes.
2. **The truth emerges slowly.** The wobble at n = 100 is still visible; the law only applies in the long run, and most clinical studies are small.
3. **Streaks happen.** Five responders in a row is not a miracle or a sign the drug is working. At p = 0.5 it is common.

## Section 4 — Two common errors of intuition

People readily see patterns, including ones that aren't there. Two errors matter clinically:

- **The gambler's fallacy**: *"four non-responders in a row; the next one's bound to respond."*
  No. Independent events are not affected by what came before. Each patient's probability is unchanged by what happens to other patients.
- **Cluster blindness**: randomness *looks* clumpy. Genuinely random sequences contain streaks and
  clusters that look deliberate. Truly "even-looking" sequences are the suspicious ones. Three MIs on
  one ward in a week, a run of treatment successes, a "cancer cluster" in one postcode: each *may*
  mean something, but this is also what chance produces routinely, and you cannot tell which by eye. Statistics can.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"After five heads, tails is due." Independent flips don't compensate; the coin doesn't know its
history. What *is* true: in the long run the proportion settles, not because future flips correct
the past, but because the past gets **diluted**. Five extra heads vanish inside ten thousand flips;
they are never "paid back".
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 5 — Why both philosophies are important

If the Bayesian meaning handles one-off facts so naturally, why does most of medical statistics run
on the frequentist one? And if frequentist methods rule the journals, why does this course include Module 5?
It's important to understand that **the two philosophies answer different questions in different ways**.

- The **frequentist** asks: *"How would this procedure behave over many repetitions?"* It refuses
  to assign probabilities to one-off facts. That's the price of objectivity in this philosophy:
  no prior beliefs enter, so the answer is the same for every analyst. Its natural
  products are statements about **procedures**. It imagines, "what would I expect if I repeated this
  study again, and again, and again", and provides all its answers in this imaginary context.
- The **Bayesian** asks: *"Given everything I know, what should I believe about this fact?"* It
  happily assigns probabilities to one-off statements ("70% that the drug works") at the price of
  declaring where the starting beliefs came from (We will address these prior beliefs, or "priors",
  in Module 5).

So when the planning meeting argued about "a 30% chance the drug really works", both were right:
that sentence is meaningless as a long-run frequency (this drug works or it doesn't), and perfectly
meaningful as a calibrated degree of belief. Most common misreadings of p-values come from mixing up these two meanings.

Frequentist analysis traditionally tells us the probability (p) of the observed data (d) if the null
hypothesis is assumed to be true (H0). We can write this as p(data | H0).

Bayesian analysis flips the inside of this equation around to give you the probability (p) of a
hypothesis (H) given the data you have observed. We can write this as p(H | data). Don't worry if this
seems confusing, we will build on it more below.

To see why the order matters so much, take the same 1000 trials and ask each question in turn.
Group the trials by the *truth* and a block reads out P(result | truth), the forward direction a
p-value reports. Regroup the identical trials by *result* and the same picture now reads out
P(truth | result), the answer you actually wanted. The two numbers can be very different, mainly depending on how common effective drugs are:

When a paper (or a colleague) makes a probability claim, ask: **which philosophy is this, and which
philosophy is the listener about to hear?** "95% confidence" (frequentist, about the procedure) is
reliably heard as "95% probability it's true" (Bayesian, about the fact). The gap between what's
said and what's heard is where much confusion arises.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 6 — A short history: why p-values answer the reverse question

If the Bayesian question — *given the data, what should I believe about the drug?* — is the more intuitive
one that clinicians actually ask, you might wonder why the statistics in your journals so rarely answer it.
The reason is partly philosophical and partly practical, and the practical reason matters most.

**Bayes came first.** The Reverend Thomas Bayes wrote down the rule for updating belief with evidence
in the eighteenth century (it was published after his death, in 1763). Laplace took the idea, developed it, and spent much of the nineteenth century using it to reason about everything from the
masses of planets to the ratio of male to female births. For a hundred years this was simply how
quantitative inference was done; they called it **inverse probability** — reasoning backwards from
the data you saw to the cause that produced it. That *is* P(hypothesis | data).

**Frequentism arrived in the 1920s and 30s**, largely as a reaction against this. Ronald Fisher gave us
significance tests and the p-value; Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson built the hypothesis-testing
framework with Type I and Type II errors, and the confidence interval. They distrusted inverse
probability because it seemed to require a **prior**, using your existing knowledge and beliefs,
and a prior seemed to bring personal opinion into what was meant to be objective science.
So they rebuilt inference to need no prior at all. That is a real intellectual achievement,
and it is why frequentist methods promise the same answer to every analyst.

There was also a practical reason frequentism took over:

<MisconceptionCallout title="It was the only thing you could realistically compute" variant="note">
To get the intuitive answer, P(hypothesis | data), Bayes' theorem makes you average over a prior.
This means for any realistic problem, solving a mathematical integral with no closed form. In 1925,
with pen, paper, and a book of printed tables, that integral was simply **not solvable**.
What *was* solvable was the other conditional: assume the null hypothesis is exactly true, work
out the distribution of the data it predicts, and read a tail area off a t-table or a z-table.
That quantity — **P(data this extreme | H₀ true)** — needs only one fixed hypothesis and a single
distribution. It is the p-value, and its main advantage in 1925 was that it could be calculated by hand.
</MisconceptionCallout>

This is the origin of the famous **transposed conditional**. You want P(H | D) — the probability
the hypothesis is true given your data. What a frequentist test gives you is P(D | H₀) — the
probability of data like yours *if a specified hypothesis, usually the null, were true*.
These are the same letters in the opposite order, and they are not interchangeable, any more than
*"probability of a rash given measles"* equals *"probability of measles given a rash."* Statistics standardised on this reversed question not because it is the one we care about, but because in the pencil-and-tables era it was the one we could **answer**. This is a major reason statistics feels unintuitive. To be fair, the approach works remarkably well given how simple its tools were.

**Then computers removed the constraint.** From the 1990s, cheap computers and simulation methods e.g., Markov
chain Monte Carlo and its relatives, made it possible to *approximate* those once-impossible
integrals to any accuracy you like, just by sampling. The intuitive answer, P(hypothesis | data),
became something a laptop can calculate. The historical reason for preferring the p-value no longer applies.

**So Bayesian methods are on the rise**, and for reasons beyond being more intuitive: they fold
in prior evidence explicitly, handle complex hierarchical and adaptive designs, and
deliver direct probability statements that should be more intuitive for clinicians to act on. Regulators have noticed; the FDA has
issued guidance for Bayesian trial designs, especially for medical devices and rare diseases where
data are scarce and using earlier evidence matters.

But the shift is **slow**, mainly because of familiarity. A century of textbooks, journal conventions,
statistics courses, and regulatory templates are built on frequentist machinery. The people reviewing the
papers and teaching the students learned it that way; the software defaults to it; the priors still make
some readers uneasy. None of that makes the old methods wrong, but it does mean the field is mid-transition,
comfortable with one approach and still learning the other. Which is exactly why a clinician today needs **both**.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Reviewer's lens" variant="lens">
When you meet a p-value, remember why it exists: it was the one conditional that could be computed by hand in 1925. It answers *"how surprising is my data if nothing is going on?"*. This is a perfectly real
question, but rarely the one you walked in with. Wanting P(hypothesis | data) is the right instinct.
It just needs a prior and a computer.
</MisconceptionCallout>
