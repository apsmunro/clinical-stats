## Section 1 — The question that comes first

Power is a *design-time* idea. Once the data are in, it's too late: the study was either big
enough or it wasn't. The most important statistical decision in a study has to be made before any data are collected, and in much clinical research it is skipped.

Recall the methodology theme from the opening of the course: *feasibility is king, science gets
shoe-horned in afterwards.* One of the most common ways that goes wrong is the underpowered study:
a trial too small to detect the effect it was looking for, which then reports a non-significant
result that gets misread as "no effect." That isn't evidence of no effect. It is an absence of evidence, produced by a study too small to find the effect.

This module shows how to avoid this: decide, up front, how big the study must be.

## Section 2 — Effect size, and the difference you must not miss (MCID)

Before you can size a study you must answer a question statistics cannot answer for you: **how big
a difference would actually matter to a patient?** This is a clinical judgement, and it has a
name, the **minimum clinically important difference (MCID)**: the *smallest* effect that is large
enough to be worth caring about. Anything below the MCID, even if it's real, isn't worth changing
practice for. Anything at or above it is something you would not want to miss.

The MCID is the most under-appreciated number in study design, and it should almost always be the
effect you **power on.** The logic is worth making explicit, because it's where most underpowered
studies go wrong:

- **Power on the MCID** and your study is sensitive enough to catch *any* effect worth having.
  A non-significant result then actually means something: "if there is any effect at all, it's
  probably too small to matter." That is a useful, interpretable null result.
- **Power on a larger, hoped-for effect** (which is tempting, because optimistic numbers demand
  smaller, cheaper samples) and you build a study that can only see *big* effects. It will
  routinely miss real, clinically worthwhile effects sitting between the MCID and your optimistic
  guess. That study is underpowered for the effects that matter.

So the discipline is simple to state and easy to skip: **decide the MCID first, then power for it.**

Where does the MCID come from? Not from statistics. It comes from clinical reasoning,
patient-important outcomes, prior literature, and sometimes formal anchor-based or
distribution-based studies. It's a judgement you must be able to *defend*, not a number you read
off your own data.

How it's expressed depends on the outcome:

- For a **continuous** outcome like BP, the MCID is a difference in means: e.g. you judge that
  5 mmHg is the smallest systolic reduction worth acting on.
- For **binary** outcomes it's a risk ratio, odds ratio, or risk difference (Module 6).
- A **standardised** effect (difference ÷ SD, i.e. "how many standard deviations apart") lets you
  compare across outcomes: Snakeoilizumab's MCID of 5 mmHg against an SD of 15 is ≈ 0.33 SD,
  a smallish but real target.

The key trade-off:
**a smaller MCID is harder to detect and needs a bigger study.** The tension between the
difference worth detecting and the sample you can feasibly recruit is the central problem in study design. Section 5 shows how this works out.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Reviewer's lens" variant="lens">
When you appraise a paper, don't just read its power statement. Find the **effect size it was
powered to detect, then ask whether that was a defensible MCID or a number chosen for convenience.**
A trial powered to detect a 15 mmHg drop has told you almost nothing about whether the drug
delivers the 5 mmHg that actually matters. "Adequately powered" is meaningless until you know
*powered for what.* As the cardiologist Professor Darrel Francis once said, "If you say a study
is underpowered but cannot say what it should have been powered for, then the only thing underpowered
is your brain".
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 3 — What statistical power actually is

Here's the definition, using the ideas from Module 4.

Suppose the drug *really does* lower BP by 5 mmHg. You run the trial. Because of sampling variation
(Module 4), you won't always get a significant result; sometimes the chance noise masks the real
effect. **Statistical power is the probability that your study comes out statistically significant
*given that the effect is genuinely there*.**

> **Power = P(significant result | the effect is real) = 1 − β**
>
> where β is the probability of *missing* a real effect (a false negative, or "Type II error").

By convention, studies aim for **80% power**, meaning that if the effect is real, you'd detect it
8 times in 10. An underpowered study at, say, 35% power is more likely to *miss* a real effect than
to find it. You wouldn't run a diagnostic test that misses the disease two-thirds of the time; an
underpowered trial is exactly that.

<MisconceptionCallout title="These thresholds are conventions, not laws">
There is nothing mathematically special about **80% power** or about **alpha = 0.05** (Module 4).
They are widely-adopted habits, not boundaries handed down by nature: Fisher proposed 0.05 as a
convenient rule of thumb, and 80% power is simply a pragmatic compromise between rigour and
feasibility. Many trials deliberately aim for **90% power when it is feasible**, accepting a larger
sample in exchange for a better chance of detecting a real effect (and a less ambiguous null). The
right level is a judgement about the cost of a missed effect versus the cost of a bigger study.
Make it deliberately; don't just inherit 80/0.05 by reflex.
</MisconceptionCallout>

We make this concrete with the same simulation engine from Module 4, but now with a *real effect
switched on*, and a significance decision applied to each simulated trial. The proportion of trials
that come out significant **is** the power.

## Section 4 — The four levers

Power depends on four linked quantities; change one and at least one other must change:

1. **Effect size**: bigger true effect → easier to detect → more power.
2. **Variability (SD)**: noisier data → harder to detect → less power. *Variance is king*, again:
   the same lever that widened the sampling distribution in Module 4 is what reduces your power here.
3. **Sample size (n)**: more patients → smaller standard error → more power. This is the lever you
   usually control.
4. **Significance threshold (alpha)**: a stricter alpha (e.g. 0.01) makes significance harder to
   reach → less power, trading false positives for false negatives.

The key idea, linking back to Module 4: **power is about how cleanly the "no effect" and
"real effect" sampling distributions separate.** Anything that pushes them apart (bigger effect,
less noise, bigger n) raises power. Drag the sliders, especially n, and watch the β region shrink
as the curves slide apart:

## Section 5 — From power to sample size

In practice you flip the question around. You *fix* the things you can argue for (**the MCID**
from Section 2, the expected SD, alpha at 0.05, power at 80%) and **solve for the sample size
n**. That's a sample-size calculation, and it's what a grant or ethics committee wants to see. The
effect you plug in should be the MCID: size the study to catch the smallest difference worth
catching.

For Snakeoilizumab (MCID 5 mmHg, SD 15, alpha 0.05, 80% power) the answer is about **142 patients per arm**: far more than the 40 the original trial used. The
original trial was badly underpowered to detect the very effect that would have mattered.

The mirror-image question is just as useful: for a sample size you're *stuck with* (limited
patients, a rare disease), what is the **minimum detectable effect (MDES)**, the smallest true
effect you'd have a fair chance of finding? Then compare it to your MCID. **If the MDES is larger
than the MCID, your study cannot reliably see the smallest effect that matters.** It is
underpowered for its clinical purpose, however large it looks.

### A worked example with a binary outcome

So far we've sized a *continuous* outcome (mean BP). Many clinical outcomes are **binary** (cured
or not, admitted or not, alive or dead) and the same logic applies with one extra complication.

Stick with Snakeoilizumab, but change the endpoint to a binary one: the **proportion of patients
achieving controlled BP** (systolic \< 140 mmHg) at 12 weeks. Suppose **40% achieve control on
placebo**. Now you state the MCID as an absolute difference: the smallest improvement worth caring
about is, say, lifting that to **55% on treatment**, a **15 percentage-point** risk difference
(equivalently a risk ratio of about 1.4). Powering for that (alpha 0.05, 80% power) needs about **170 patients per arm**. Try it in the calculator above: switch the outcome toggle to *binary
proportions*.

The complication: for a binary outcome, **the required sample size depends on the baseline event
rate, not just the size of the difference.** The variability of a proportion is largest near 50%
and shrinks towards 0% or 100%, so the *same* absolute MCID costs a different number of patients
depending on where the baseline sits. A 15-point difference from a 5% baseline (5% → 20%) needs a
very different sample from the same 15 points around 40% → 55%. And **rare events are expensive**:
outcomes that happen to few patients demand large samples or long follow-up, which is exactly why
event-driven trials (e.g. mortality) are so big. The practical lesson connects straight back to the
fallback options below: if your binary outcome is rare, a more common (but still patient-important)
endpoint, or a composite, may be the honest way to reach feasible power.

### When you can't afford to power on the MCID

This happens often in real studies. Powering on a small
MCID often demands a sample you simply cannot recruit. When that happens, **do not quietly inflate
the effect size to a number you can afford**. That hides an underpowered study behind a respectable-looking calculation. Instead, work through the legitimate options, roughly in order of
preference:

1. **Increase feasibility**: multi-centre recruitment, longer enrolment, collaborative networks,
   registry or routine-data designs.
2. **Reduce the variance**: a more precise or less noisy outcome measure, repeated measures, or a
   more homogeneous population shrinks the SD and so shrinks the required n (*variance is king*,
   again).
3. **Rethink the outcome**: choose a different endpoint where a clinically meaningful change is
   *larger relative to its variability* (a better signal-to-noise ratio), or a more responsive
   measure. Often the smartest move is changing *what* you measure, not how many patients you chase.
4. **Reconsider whether the study should run at all**: if no honest design can detect the MCID,
   the ethical answer may be not to expose patients to a study that cannot answer its question, and
   to redirect the effort.

The unifying rule: **the MCID is fixed by clinical reality, not by your budget.** When feasibility
and the MCID collide, you change the design. Never the definition of what matters.

## Section 6 — Why underpowered studies are worse than you think

A weak study isn't just "less likely to find things." It causes harm in two ways:

**1. It misses real effects.** The obvious harm: a genuinely effective treatment is tested in a
study too small to detect it, gets a non-significant result, and is wrongly written off. Patients
lose a treatment that worked.

**2. It exaggerates the effects it does find (the winner's curse).** This is the counter-intuitive
one. In an underpowered study, the *only* way to cross the significance line is to get a sampling
fluke that happens to inflate the estimate. So the results that reach significance systematically *overstate* the true effect, sometimes by a lot, occasionally with the wrong sign
entirely. (These are sometimes called **Type M**, magnitude, and **Type S**, sign, errors.)
Underpowered literatures are therefore full of exciting effect sizes that shrink or vanish on
replication.

See it for yourself. The true effect below is fixed at 5 mmHg. At n = 30 the "published"
(significant) trials average well above 5. Drag n up and watch the inflation collapse toward the
truth:

<WinnerCurseDemo trueEffect={5} sd={15} defaultN={30} seed={6001} />

So an underpowered study is likely to mislead either way: false reassurance if the result is null, an inflated effect if it is significant. This is a genuine **research-ethics**
issue, not just a statistical nicety: enrolling patients in a study that cannot answer its question
exposes them to risk for no scientific gain.

<MisconceptionCallout title="Common misconception">
"My study was non-significant, so the treatment doesn't work." A non-significant result from an
underpowered study is *uninformative*, not evidence of no effect. Absence of evidence is not
evidence of absence, especially when you never had the power to find it. This goes all the way
back to understanding what a p value represents — *how surprising is this data given I assumed there
is no true difference?* If you understand a p value is p(data | H0) it stops you falling into the trap
of assuming a large p value always implies there is no true difference between groups.
</MisconceptionCallout>

## Section 7 — Power pitfalls

<MisconceptionCallout title="The post-hoc power fallacy">
Calculating "observed power" *after* the study from the observed effect tells you nothing new.
It's just a re-expression of the p-value, and it's circular. Power must be computed from the effect
you *care about* (a clinical judgement), set *before* the study. Reviewers who ask for post-hoc
power on a non-significant result are asking the wrong question; the right tools are confidence
intervals (Module 4) and a pre-specified design.
</MisconceptionCallout>

Two more, briefly:

- **Powering above the MCID** (choosing a larger, more convenient effect than the smallest one
  worth detecting) is the most common cause of hidden underpowering: the calculation looks fine, but the study cannot detect the effect that matters. Always power on the MCID, and
  if you can't, be transparent about the gap rather than hiding it in the effect size.
- **Ignoring attrition and clustering.** Real sample sizes must inflate for dropout, and account
  for non-independence (e.g. cluster trials). Naive n is a floor, not the final answer.

<CollapsibleRSnippet
  caption="Power by simulation (the same logic as the interactive widget)"
  code={`set.seed(123)
sim_power <- function(n, true_diff = 5, sd = 15, alpha = 0.05, reps = 5000) {
  hits <- replicate(reps, {
    control      <- rnorm(n, 120,             sd)
    intervention <- rnorm(n, 120 - true_diff, sd)
    t.test(intervention, control)$p.value < alpha
  })
  mean(hits)               # estimated power
}
sim_power(40)              # ~0.35  -> badly underpowered
sim_power(142)             # ~0.80`}
/>
